The sources dedicate significant attention to the **Challenges and Limitations** of Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED), particularly within the context of Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) and adaptive learning platforms. While acknowledging the transformative benefits of AI, the material emphasizes that successful integration requires addressing critical issues related to implementation costs, ethical concerns, data security, algorithmic integrity, and the preservation of the essential human element in education.

In the larger context of AIED, these challenges underscore the necessity for robust policy, transparent design, and sustained human oversight to ensure that technology enhances, rather than undermines, learning and equity.

### 1. High Costs and Resource Inequality

A primary barrier to the widespread, equitable adoption of AIED technologies is the financial burden associated with development and implementation.

*   **Development Costs:** Building robust ITS requires **considerable investment in research and technology**. Creating the necessary interactive content and sophisticated adaptive algorithms is **expensive and resource-intensive**.
*   **Implementation Costs:** Beyond initial development, implementing AI technologies demands significant **infrastructure investment, technology support staff, and continuous professional development**.
*   **Deepening the Digital Divide:** These financial barriers mean that under-resourced schools and districts cannot afford the necessary investments. The result is that AI-powered learning remains mostly accessible to the rich, risking the **widening of gaps among digital haves and have-nots**. This inequality may lead to "digital redlining in education," where unequal access compounds existing educational disparities. UNESCO cautions that this difference in access could worsen existing educational disparities, particularly in the Global South.

### 2. Pedagogical and Cognitive Risks

The nature of AI-driven adaptive learning introduces specific risks to the learning process itself, particularly concerning critical thinking and dependency.

*   **Risk of Over-Reliance and Disengagement:** Over-reliance on AI is a critical limitation, as it can potentially **reduce valuable face-to-face interactions** between students and teachers. This over-reliance can also lead to **diminished critical thinking skills**, **cognitive disengagement**, and **learner passivity**. Students and teachers may use AI tools **"as shortcuts to success with little or no effort,"** which undermines the development of critical skills.
*   **The Augmentation Paradox:** AI tools may foster performance in the short term, but simultaneously **delay and stop the development of capacities for independent learning in the long run**. This is referred to as the "augmentation paradox".
*   **Suitability for Subject Matter:** ITS are not suitable for every subject. While effective for well-defined, structured subjects like **math or coding**, the **complexity and variability of topics in humanities or creative fields** make developing an effective ITS more difficult. Furthermore, ITS for **ill-defined problems** (e.g., legal argumentation or dispute resolution) face challenges because the lack of structure makes it difficult to model, provide appropriate feedback, or scaffold effective learning pathways.
*   **Inaccuracy and Misinformation:** AI systems are "only as good as the algorithms it is based on". If training data is biased or inaccurate, the AI may create **misinformation or contradictory content**.

### 3. Erosion of the Human Element (The Paradox of Connection)

A critical limitation of AI is its inability to replicate the social and emotional aspects of human teaching, leading to the **"paradox of connection"**.

*   **Lack of Emotional Intelligence:** ITS may struggle with nuances such as **emotional intelligence**, often **lacking the personal touch that human tutors can provide**. This limitation may impact students’ ability to develop soft skills or receive the emotional support that a teacher offers. AI systems lack understanding of emotions, motivations, and social contexts that humans naturally perceive.
*   **Reduced Relationships:** The assumption that AI will "free up time" for teachers to focus on meaningful interactions is challenged by research showing the opposite is often true in practice. Half of the students in one study agreed that **using AI in class makes them feel *less connected* to their teachers**. High AI usage has been linked to **increased loneliness** when students perceive the technology as their primary source of support, as the technology inherently "lacks empathy".
*   **Social and Holistic Development:** The face-to-face interactions reduced by over-reliance on AI are **vital for holistic development**, including fostering social skills and building rapport.

### 4. Ethical Risks: Bias, Transparency, and Privacy

The core mechanisms that enable AI (data collection and algorithms) also introduce severe ethical challenges related to justice, equity, and trust.

*   **Algorithmic Bias:** Predictive models trained on historical educational data can **reflect or reinforce existing biases**, potentially **amplifying existing educational inequalities**. For instance, studies show models reinforcing racial inequities, where Black students often receive fewer resources when algorithms identify them as "at-risk". Furthermore, bias has been identified against **non-native English speakers**, whose writing samples are frequently misclassified as AI-generated, leading to false accusations of plagiarism.
*   **Lack of Transparency (Opacity):** Lack of transparency in AI decision-making **will erode trust** and raise ethical questions regarding surveillance and consent in education. When there is **no transparency in decision-making**, educators and learners will find **interpreting or trusting system outputs much more difficult**. The use of opaque neural network approaches makes it difficult to decipher how a decision has been made.
*   **Data Privacy and Security:** AI systems require an **unprecedented amount of data** about student behaviour, performance, and learning patterns, creating **significant data privacy risks**. Data collected for personalised learning may be misused for non-educational purposes by edtech companies. Continuous monitoring also creates **surveillance concerns** and psychological effects where students feel watched.
*   **Academic Integrity:** AI presents **"new challenges to academic integrity,"** with cheating and plagiarism being chief concerns raised by educators.

### 5. Implementation and User Readiness Barriers

Even when AI is available, technical and human factors can hinder its effective use.

*   **Teacher Preparedness (AI Competency):** Many teachers lack the necessary **training in AI technologies** and the pedagogical approaches required for AI-enhanced learning. Educators need to understand how AI-powered systems work, interpret analytics dashboards, and develop the ethical reasoning to model responsible AI use for students. Without proper training, teachers may not understand what adaptive systems do or how to connect them to curricular goals.
*   **Student Digital Literacy:** Students, especially those from **less technology-privileged backgrounds**, may have difficulty **navigating AI interfaces and optimizing parameters**. This assumption of digital fluency creates educational divides behind the mask of personalisation.
*   **Technical Glitches and Inconsistency:** Students face irregularities when using AI tools, finding them slick for structured tasks (like test prep) but often **falling short on open-ended assignments or creative synthesis**. This inconsistency suggests the usefulness of AI depends heavily on the task type, the learner's ability, and contextual factors.

In summary, the sources emphasize that while AIED promises accessibility and improved outcomes, these gains are contingent on institutions and practitioners actively mitigating the risks of cost inequality, ethical and algorithmic bias, and the insidious potential for **substituting** human connection with technological expediency.
