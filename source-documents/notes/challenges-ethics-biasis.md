# Challenges, Ethics, and Bias of AI in Education

### Lack of Human Interaction
A key concern is that a reliance on AI may reduce meaningful student-teacher engagement, which is vital for mentorship and support. AI systems often struggle with the nuances of emotional intelligence and lack the "personal touch" that human tutors provide. This can negatively impact a student's ability to develop essential soft skills or receive the emotional support a teacher can offer. This "risk of over-reliance" on technology can reduce valuable face-to-face interactions and "inadvertently diminish the role of educators".

### Privacy, Data, and Ethical Concerns
The collection and storage of extensive student data raise significant ethical and security issues. These systems require large-scale learner data, which may include sensitive information. This creates a primary challenge around data privacy, informed consent, and the transparency of the AI's decision-making process. There is a fundamental conflict in needing to access student data for personalisation while also protecting that data from misuse.

### Bias in Algorithms
AI systems can contain biases that systematically disadvantage specific student groups. The "FairAIED" article classifies these biases into three main levels of harm:

#### 1. Group-level Biases
These are systemic inequalities affecting groups based on race, gender, or socioeconomic status.
* **Historical Bias:** Reinforces past educational inequalities found in training data.
* **Mapping Bias:** Occurs when a model trained on one group (e.g., urban schools) is inaccurately applied to another (e.g., rural schools).
* **Representation Bias:** Results from certain groups being underrepresented in the training data (e.g., essay graders trained only on native English-speaking students).
* **Exclusion Bias:** The design of an AI tool systematically marginalizes a group (e.g., a platform lacking accessibility features for students with disabilities).

#### 2. Individual-level Biases
This bias arises when the AI treats students with similar abilities or profiles differently.
* **Measurement Bias:** Occurs when the data does not accurately measure a student's true ability (e.g., penalizing non-Standard American English).
* **Confirmation Bias:** The algorithm tends to recommend content that aligns with a student's existing strengths, limiting exposure to new subjects.

#### 3. Multi-level Biases
A category for biases that interact across both individual and group levels.
* **Learning Bias:** When prioritizing predictive accuracy inadvertently amplifies disparities in outcomes for different groups.
* **Interaction Bias:** Disparities in how students engage with a platform lead to biased outcomes (e.g., high-engagement students get better personalisation).
* **Stereotyping Bias:** The AI makes assumptions based on societal stereotypes (e.g., recommending careers based on gender).
* **Censored Outcome Bias:** Inaccuracies that arise from incomplete student data (e.g., a student transfers, so their graduation status is unknown), which can disproportionately affect marginalized groups.

### Dependence on Technology
An over-reliance on AI may hinder the development of students' critical thinking and problem-solving skills. Some research notes that traditional methods, like discussion, remain superior for fostering deep critical thinking. This over-reliance could limit the development of reflective and independent learning, making it crucial to maintain a "balance between technology and human oversight".

### Infrastructure Readiness and the Digital Divide
The promise of AI as an equalizer is threatened by the risk that it will "deepen existing inequalities". This "digital divide" represents a substantial challenge. Many institutions face limitations in internet connectivity, hardware availability, and technical support, which inhibits the successful deployment of sophisticated AI systems. Disparities in access to digital infrastructure can aggravate existing inequities, as not all students have equal access to AI tools or the skills to use them effectively.

### Lack of Educator Training
A significant barrier to effective integration is the "lack of comprehensive training for educators". Teachers often feel unprepared to navigate AI tools or align them with instructional goals, leading to underutilization or misapplication. This creates a "growing digital divide" not just among students, but between students who have adopted the technology and faculty who are less comfortable with it.

### Curriculum and Assessment Integration
Challenges also exist in integrating AI-generated content into existing curricula. AI may not be suitable for every subject; for example, while it works well for structured topics like math or coding, its effectiveness in the "humanities or creative fields" is less clear. This challenge requires educators to "rethink the entire educational process", moving beyond traditional essays to new forms of assessment like oral defenses, project-based learning, and reflective journals.
